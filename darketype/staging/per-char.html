per-character chromatic displacement

The membrane responds to pressure. Each letter exists independently within the substrate, held in place by surface tension until an external force disturbs the equilibrium. When proximity increases, characters displace outward along the pressure vector, trailing chromatic fringe behind them like light through a defective prism.

This is not animation in the decorative sense. It is physics applied to typography — the same force model that governs fluid displacement, scaled down to the glyph level. Every character carries its own inertia, its own resistance to being moved, its own chromatic signature when forced out of alignment.

the displacement model

Characters within the proximity radius experience a radial push proportional to their distance from the cursor. The closest glyphs displace the most, up to six pixels of translation. Each displaced character tilts slightly — a micro-rotation that suggests physical torque rather than digital transform. The chromatic fringe splits along the push vector: red forward, blue backward, green ghosting at peak intensity.

The effect dissolves when pressure releases. Activity decay ensures that the membrane relaxes back to equilibrium over roughly one and a half seconds. There is no snap-back, no bounce — just viscous return, each character finding its way home through damped motion.

wavefront propagation

Click events generate expanding wavefronts that interact with individual characters rather than blocks. The ring passes through text at a controlled velocity, displacing each letter as the leading edge crosses its position. Behind the front, a dissolving wake leaves characters temporarily displaced before they settle. Gravity acts on the wavefront over time — the initially radial displacement rotates downward, creating a drip effect that increases with the ripple's age.

  • Wavefront band width: sixty pixels at character resolution
  • Peak displacement at the front: eight pixels outward
  • Wake displacement behind: three pixels, fading with distance
  • Gravity rotation blends radial to vertical over the ripple lifetime
  • Ripple displacement stacks additively with proximity displacement

spatial culling

Performance requires that we do not compute transforms for every character on every frame. A two-tier system gates the work: first, block-level bounding rectangles reject entire paragraphs that fall outside the proximity radius. Then, within active blocks, individual character distances are checked against cached positions. The result is that only two to six hundred characters are evaluated per frame, even when the page contains thousands.

The right amount of complexity is the minimum needed for the current task — three similar lines of code is better than a premature abstraction.

What matters is not the sophistication of the algorithm but the fidelity of the result. Each character should feel like a physical object responding to real force. The math is simple — distance, angle, intensity curve — but the accumulated effect of hundreds of independently displaced glyphs creates something that reads as material rather than digital.
