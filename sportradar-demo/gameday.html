When Every Second Counts:
Game-Day Reliability

Featured Incident Response · Operational Risk

Saturday afternoon. Premier League matchday. Sportradar is feeding real-time data to betting operators powering millions of in-play bets per second. A data pipeline starts degrading. What happens next defines whether Sportradar keeps a contract — or loses one.

The Setup

Sportradar processes data from 900,000 live sporting events annually. They have 500 engineers organized into 17 autonomous tribes, each with the freedom to choose their own tooling. They serve 800 betting operators and 900+ media partners across 80 countries.

They're already doing platform engineering well — they call it "paved roads." They've standardized on New Relic for observability, they have an internal managed services catalog, and they measure every service against availability, reliability, and performance SLOs.

But here's where it breaks down.

The Scenario

Imagine the pipeline processing live match events from Anfield starts degrading. Data to betting operators slows. Odds become stale. In-play markets can't be priced accurately.

This is not hypothetical.

With 900,000+ live events per year, 17 tribes deploying independently, and betting operators requiring sub-second data delivery in regulated markets — pipeline degradation during a live event is a matter of when, not if.

What Happens Today

T+0:00

Alert fires in New Relic

New Relic tells you what's broken — but not who owns it or what changed recently.

T+0:02

"Anyone know who owns match-events-ingest?"

Someone posts in Slack. Three tribes think it might be theirs. Two are wrong.

T+0:20

Owner found — but what changed?

The actual owning team (Tribe 7) starts investigating. Manual cross-referencing: GitLab pipelines, config history, deployment logs. They discover a config change was deployed an hour ago by a different tribe's service that feeds into theirs.

T+0:35

Cross-tribe coordination begins

Manual coordination between Tribe 7 and Tribe 3. Three different dashboards. Slack threads across multiple channels. Tribal knowledge is the only map.

T+0:45

Rollback completed. Service restored.

45 minutes during a live Premier League match.

The Cost of 45 Minutes

Regulatory Exposure

Betting operators received stale data — a compliance violation in licensed markets. Regulators in the UK, EU, and US hold operators accountable for data accuracy.

Financial Losses

Operators couldn't price in-play bets accurately. Financial losses get passed to Sportradar as trust erosion. With 122% net retention, trust is the business model.

Contract Risk

The Premier League reviews Sportradar's reliability for their next contract renewal. Every major partnership — NBA through 2030, MLB through 2032 — depends on operational trust.

Deployment Fear

Internal teams lose confidence in the deployment process. Next time they'll be afraid to deploy on match days — slowing innovation when speed is a competitive advantage.

The Root Cause

This isn't a monitoring problem — they have New Relic. It isn't a deployment problem — they have CI/CD. It's a context problem.

When the alert fires, nobody has a single view that connects:

Question Where the Answer Lives
Which service is affected? New Relic + internal wiki
Who owns it? GitLab CODEOWNERS + Slack tribal knowledge
What was deployed recently? GitLab pipeline history
What other services depend on it? Architecture diagrams (maybe outdated)
Does it meet production-readiness standards? Internal scorecard system
Who's on-call for that tribe? PagerDuty / team calendar

The information exists across 6 different tools. Stitching it together takes 20 minutes — which is 20 minutes too long during a live event.

A data pipeline failure during integrity monitoring isn't just a tech issue — it's regulatory and legal exposure. The FBI is on the World Cup Task Force. When Sportradar's systems are the line of defense against match-fixing at the world's most-watched sporting event, "we had a 45-minute outage" is not an acceptable answer.
