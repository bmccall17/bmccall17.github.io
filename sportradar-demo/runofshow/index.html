Act 1: Platform Engineering & Why Port

5 min

"Before I get into the specific use case, I want to share how I think about platform engineering — because it's not just something I've studied. It's something I've lived, even when I didn't call it that."

"Platform engineering is what happens when you stop asking developers to be infrastructure experts and start giving them guardrails instead of gates.

Here's what I mean. In most engineering organizations, developers don't just write code — they also have to figure out how to deploy it, how to monitor it, how to secure it, how to get it through compliance. They're context-switching between writing features and fighting infrastructure. That's not a productivity problem — it's a design problem.

Platform engineering solves this by building an internal platform — a curated set of tools, templates, and self-service workflows — that lets developers focus on what they're good at: building features. The platform team handles the 'how do I deploy this?' and 'how do I know it's healthy?' questions by building golden paths: opinionated, paved roads that encode best practices into the default workflow.

Think of it like a highway system. You can still take back roads, but the highway gets you there faster, safer, and with fewer decisions."

"But here's the thing that gets missed: building the platform isn't the hard part. Getting developers to actually use it is.

This is where developer experience — DevEx — comes in. DevEx is the measure of how much friction a developer encounters in their daily work. Not just 'is the tool available?' but 'does it feel natural to use? Does it save me time? Does it give me confidence that I'm doing the right thing?'

I've spent my career on exactly this problem. At BTU, we built tools from scratch because they didn't exist — and then the real work was getting 6,000 enterprise users to actually adopt them. We didn't succeed by mandating usage. We succeeded by making the tools so useful that people chose them. Onboarding rituals that built confidence. Champions who led by example. Health dashboards that showed teams their own progress.

The lesson applies directly to platform engineering: the best internal developer portal in the world is worthless if developers don't trust it. And trust comes from experience — the first time a developer uses a self-service action and it just works, that's the moment adoption starts."

"Port is the layer that makes platform engineering real for developers.

The software catalog gives every team a shared map of what exists — who owns what, what's deployed where, what's healthy and what isn't. Self-service actions turn 'file a ticket and wait' into 'click a button and it's done.' Scorecards create a maturity framework that's transparent — teams can see where they stand and what they need to do to level up. And the AI layer means the platform can start answering questions and taking action autonomously.

But the key insight — and what I think Port gets right — is orchestration. Port doesn't replace your CI/CD, your monitoring, your security scanner. It connects them into a coherent experience. That's the difference between a tool and a platform."

"So with that framing, let me show you what this looks like for a real company. Sportradar has 500 engineers in 17 autonomous tribes, processing data from 900,000 live sporting events a year. They're already doing platform engineering — paved roads, scorecards, centralized observability. But they have gaps that Port can fill. Let me walk you through the use case."

Port doesn't replace your tools — it connects them. And for a company like Sportradar, where 45 minutes of confusion during a live Premier League match means regulatory exposure and lost contracts, that connection is worth everything.

"Sportradar processes data from 900,000 live sporting events annually. They serve 800 betting operators and 900 media partners across 80 countries.

But here's where it breaks down. Imagine it's a Saturday afternoon. Premier League matchday. Sportradar is feeding real-time data to betting operators powering millions of in-play bets per second. A data pipeline — the one processing live match events from Anfield — starts degrading.

Today, here's what happens:

  1. An alert fires in New Relic. But New Relic tells you what's broken, not who owns it or what changed recently.
  2. Someone posts in Slack: 'Anyone know who owns the match-events-ingest service?'
  3. Three tribes think it might be theirs. Two are wrong. Twenty minutes lost.
  4. The actual owning team finds the issue: a config change deployed an hour ago by a different tribe's service.
  5. They roll back. Manual coordination across two tribes, three dashboards, deployment logs in GitLab.
  6. Total time: 45 minutes. During a live Premier League match.

The business impact:

  • Betting operators received stale data — regulatory exposure in licensed markets
  • Operators couldn't price in-play bets accurately — financial losses
  • The Premier League reviews Sportradar's reliability for contract renewal
  • Internal teams lose confidence — afraid to deploy on match days

The Root Cause: "This isn't a monitoring problem — they have New Relic. It isn't a deployment problem — they have CI/CD. It's a context problem. When the alert fires, nobody has a single view that connects: which service is affected, who owns it, what was deployed recently, what other services depend on it, whether it meets production readiness standards, who's on-call for that tribe. The information exists across 6 different tools. Stitching it together takes 20 minutes."

context problem not monitoring problem 6 different tools 45 min MTTR during live event regulatory exposure

"Now let me show you what this looks like with Port.

Same scenario. Saturday. Premier League. Pipeline degrading at Anfield.

  1. New Relic detects the anomaly. Port's automation triggers — the incident is already contextualized.
  2. Port's software catalog answers instantly: this is match-events-ingest, owned by Tribe 7 (Live Data), on-call is Maria Chen.
  3. The dashboard shows: a deployment was made 47 minutes ago by feed-normalization in Tribe 3. Scorecard shows it was at Silver tier — it hadn't met Gold-tier deployment gating.
  4. Port's self-service action fires: 'Create Incident Channel' pulls in Maria, the Tribe 3 deployer, and the SRE lead. Context posted automatically.
  5. Maria identifies the issue, rolls back via a self-service action in Port.
  6. Total time: 8 minutes. During a live Premier League match.

What changed? The information was already connected. Port didn't replace New Relic, GitLab, or Slack. It orchestrated them into a coherent workflow."
